
"The Bee's Knees" is a repository focused on solving a key problem with beekeeping.

This repository was developed to show adequate documentation of the development of my project for the purpose of scholarship technologies AS93601.

Problem Statement:

Current beekeeping technology is designed for commercial utilization, where honey extraction is prioritized over colony health. Most beginners do not understand the implications that come with using the commercial hive designs.

The lack of technology surrounding beginner beekeepers leaves them without support or ecological balance. Because of this paradigm, beginners tend to manage their beehive in a way that compromises its health and integrity. This inconsistency leads to three major problems, which are time management and organization, hive viability, and honey bias.

Honey bias (the overextraction of honey):

Honey has important enzymes and vitamins that help protect the bees from viruses and mites; the alternative sugar syrups do not substitute the enzymes effectively.

Because the standardized hive designs are centered around commercial use, beginners struggle with how much honey they must take without shifting the ecosystem of the colony. As a result, new beekeepers end up taking too much honey, leaving the colony in a less sustainable position going into winter.

Hive’s viability:

The commercially designed hives do not have Poka-Yoke (mistake-proof) features that stop beginners from making common mistakes that directly affect the colony's viability in a negative way. Additionally, the hives are also developed to be used with specialized, expensive machinery that regularly costs as much as the hive itself, making beekeeping less accessible for beginners with smaller operations.

Time management and organization:

In the world of beginner beekeeping, many designs, such as the Langstroth hive, have barriers of time commitment and organization. Many people today have full schedules that make it difficult to put the time aside to look after a hive correctly, especially when there are no reminders set in place, like a calendar or notification system.

Goal:

To develop an accessible beehive utilizing current-day technology and procedures that promotes a balanced relationship between the user and the colony. The final beehive must target the three main problems of time management and organization, the hive's viability, and honey bias.

Target Audience:

New beekeepers who are looking at buying a first beehive with low skills or knowledge.

Specifications:

Regenerative Design (designing in a way to generate net-positive impacts for both people and nature):

- At least 90% of the materials by mass must be regenerative and sustainable (calculated by mass, where all parts will be added to a master list).

- All structural components of the beehive must be fully recyclable or biodegradable.

- All final resources must be researched to ensure they are ethically sourced. (Rigorous background checks on fair wages, environmental impacts, and business ethics.)

- When developing new technology, the health of the hive must be considered over the accessibility of the user.

- All basic procedures, such as hive inspections, must take no more than twice as long as the Langstroth hive, excluding extraction time. (This metric will be based on timed trials on both hive designs.)

- Any technology to do with the development of a beehive must support the bees in a passive manner, where the bees don't rely on the hive.

Hive viability:

- The beehive must consider the natural environment of bees and have at least three measurable improvements over the Langstroth hive that support the bees' viability with minimal negative impact. (E.g., energy conservation, improved varroa mite mitigation, or improved hive humidity management)

- All materials that bees directly or indirectly come in contact with must be 100% bee-safe. (Cross-referenced with the 2014 food grade standard)

- The beehive must take advantage of poka-yoke features, so the user is less susceptible to making mistakes, such as flipping frames or crushing the queen, especially if the design is not as intuitive as the standards. (Some examples include single-direction frames and labeled components.)

Accessible:

- The final beehive must have a total batch production price of no more than 500 NZD by incorporating only high-value features. (The cost of labor will not be considered in this metric, as the hives are designed for mass manufacturing, where time is less relevant.)

- The final beehive must include any proprietary technology that I have designed in its overall package and budget that is required for base functionality.

- The final beehive must be flat packable for cheaper shipping, so more money goes into more important development than expensive shipping. (Fastening pre-tapped screws and gluing features are both post-shipping operations and are both permitted; no operations that require more time or complexity are to be included.)

- The final beehive must consider the user range of both the users with and without experience in its construction. (Referenced via the Wakatipu Beekeeper Club and the high school.)

- All proprietary technology must have valid reasoning supported with evidence that demonstrates its strengths over standardized technology.

Time Management and Organization:

- The beehive must have integrated passive technology that promotes the regular intervention of the user rather than replaces them. (This metric will be analyzed by gathering a range of user feedback on how new features influenced their organization and time management.)

- The beehive must have a python/JavaScript app developed alongside it to help provide additional information for the user about their hive and general information.

Other:

- Any unsafe aspects of the hive must be clearly documented. (Labels and warnings with additional on-app context)

- Every new development must undergo evaluation to support its claims with concrete metric-based proof of overall improvement and consideration of drawbacks. (Metric-based proof includes final real-world testing.)

- The final hive must match the aesthetics of a range of expected environments effectively. (Real-world setting comparisons and feedback)

- When batch constructed, each hive must take less than 16 hours' worth of manual labor to manufacture. (One hive can take more than a day as long as it takes no more than 16 hours to produce.)
